# Review of earlier conclusions (2026-11-17)

Written after checking every failing training sample against the policy, the database and Amazon's corrected τ²-bench ([FIXES.md](https://github.com/amazon-agi/tau2-bench-verified/blob/main/FIXES.md)). The suspect list and the reasons are in [`../suspect_samples.json`](../suspect_samples.json).

## What we got wrong

1. **"Over-refusal is the biggest lever" was wrong.** The [20 × 4 analysis](2026-11-16T16-48-39_tau2-airline_20x4_analysis.md) blamed failures on samples 7, 9, 14, 23 and 27 on the agent refusing allowed actions. In fact the expected answer is wrong in all five: it contradicts the policy (7, 9, 27), uses passengers that aren't in the database (23), or contradicts itself (14). In most of these the agent's refusal was **correct**.
2. **Sample 33 was not an agent bag-fee mistake.** The customer is Gold in economy, so the policy allows 3 free bags. The agent's 0 paid bags was right, and the expected answer (2 paid) is wrong.
3. **Sample 5 was described too strongly** as a "wrong compensation offer". The customer is a regular member but flies business, so the policy *does* allow a delay certificate after a change or cancellation. What the agent did is better described as nudging the customer to cancel a $28,892 booking to get a $200 certificate, which v1's guidance discourages. Amazon fixes this sample by telling the customer not to change or cancel.
4. **Samples 38 and 39 were unexamined failures.** Both expected answers contradict the policy: 38 expects a delay certificate without a change or cancellation, and 39 expects a cancellation that no rule allows.
5. **Sample 21 has a disputed expected payment card.** Amazon changes both the request and the card.

## What still holds

- The port's grader is what we optimise. Target items (the Answer field) are for diagnosis only.
- Genuine agent mistakes in training: the bag fee in sample 17 (charged for a bag that should be free), and the payment-rule mix-up in sample 14. Sample 14 fails anyway because of its own data, but the rule mix-up was real and v1 fixed it.
- Results within about ±0.1 are within run-to-run noise.

## Where training stands

On the 30 training samples × 4 epochs ([comparison](2026-11-16_train_v0_vs_v1.txt)):

| | Baseline (port's prompt) | v1 |
|---|---|---|
| All 30: pass^1 / pass^4 | 0.68 / 0.60 | 0.72 / 0.70 |
| Excluding the 9 suspects (21 samples): pass^1 / pass^4 | 0.95 / 0.86 | **1.00 / 1.00** |

v1 passes every non-suspect training sample in every epoch, so training has nothing left to tune. **No v2 was written:** the three samples it was meant to target (14, 23, 39) are all suspect, and tuning for them would mean teaching the agent to break the policy.

## Caveats about v1

- Part of v1 ("refuse only when a specific rule forbids it") was motivated by the over-refusal diagnosis in point 1, which was wrong. It caused no regressions in training (samples where the agent must refuse still pass), but it is the line most likely to backfire on new samples.
- 21 samples at 100% is a ceiling: training can no longer distinguish between prompt versions. The test set is now the only informative measure.

## Checked and fine

- No conversation hit the 100-round limit (longest: 25 messages), so no sample was graded on an unfinished conversation.
- No hidden errors from the port's swallowed exceptions in any run.
- With prompt v0, the custom task's agent prompt is identical to the port's, so the baseline combining both runs is valid.
